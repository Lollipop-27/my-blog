# Project 2 — UX of Latency

## Week 3: From Digital Delays to Observable Behaviour

### Research Direction: Measured Time vs. Experienced Time

**Monday, October 5**

Our initial discussion focused on the relationship between measured time and experienced time. While measured time can be recorded objectively in seconds, experienced time is subjective and influenced by the situation and the person waiting. A short delay may feel insignificant in one context but frustrating in another.

This distinction became the foundation of our research into the UX of latency. Rather than investigating latency purely as a technical issue, we wanted to understand how delays influence people's experiences and behaviour when interacting with digital systems.

### Bodystorming and Initial Observations

**Tuesday, October 6**

We used bodystorming to explore three examples of digital latency: Face ID, Flywire payments, and metro gates. Each example presented a different interaction in which users depend on a system to respond before they can continue with their task.

Following our tutorial with Tanisha, we reconsidered how we could investigate the relationship between waiting time and user experience. Since the perception of time varies between individuals, we needed to consider context and observable behaviour rather than relying only on the duration of a delay.

I suggested investigating school entrance gates, where students scan their IDs to gain access. During busy periods, queues may form, creating situations in which delays affect the flow of people. We visited the gates as a group to observe the interaction between users and the system.

### Refining the Research Approach

**Wednesday–Thursday, October 7–8**

Our group discussions focused on clarifying the distinction between digital latency and physical latency. We began considering how a delay originating in a digital system can create physical consequences, such as interrupted movement, queues, and changes in people's actions.

Feedback from the tutorial highlighted the importance of behavioural observation. Instead of assuming that waiting necessarily causes frustration, we needed to investigate what observable signs might suggest impatience or annoyance. These could become areas for further observation, rather than conclusions made in advance.

We also decided to broaden our research from school gates to gates and checkout systems more generally. This gave us a wider range of everyday interactions to investigate. We agreed to conduct individual research before meeting again.

### Behavioural Mapping in Retail Environments

**Friday–Saturday, October 9–10**

During a team call on Friday, we coordinated our next steps and arranged a behavioural mapping session for Saturday.

We began at Sainsbury's, observing its self-service kiosks, and also tried using a coffee machine that presented some technical difficulties. We then visited Tesco Express, Morrisons, and Lidl to examine different checkout environments.

These locations provided opportunities to investigate how people interact with systems that control the pace of a task. We were particularly interested in moments when an interaction is interrupted, when users need to wait, or when they must change their approach because a system does not work as expected.

The next step is to organise our observations and compare behaviours across these environments. This will help us identify patterns in how people respond to delays and consider how the relationship between digital latency, physical movement, and perceived waiting time could inform our design direction.
