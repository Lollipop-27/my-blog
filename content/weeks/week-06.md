# Project 3 — Weekly Weblog: UX of Compost

## Week 6: From Research to Material Experimentation

### Creative Toolkit

**Monday, October 26**

We developed our creative toolkit by arranging a selection of food items for both humans and compost on a table. We asked participants to create two meals: one for a human and another for compost. Through this activity, we wanted to explore how people understand the needs of both and whether they approach the two in different ways.

An interesting observation was that participants seemed to prioritise healthy choices regardless of who or what they were preparing the meal for. This raised questions about how people understand food, nutrients and what makes something suitable for composting.

I also invited my flatmate, who was not part of our class, to try the activity. I wanted to hear a perspective from someone outside our immediate group, as this could help us identify assumptions we might otherwise overlook.

### Developing the Experiment

**Tuesday, October 27**

During our tutorial with Tanisha, we discussed how to develop the project further. One suggestion was to connect human feelings with compost, while another was to think about what compost itself could teach us. This encouraged us to reconsider the relationship between people and compost, rather than treating compost simply as a subject to explain.

As a group, we agreed to create an experiment that would demonstrate how different inputs could affect the final compost product. This gave us a clearer direction for moving from our initial research and creative toolkit towards a more tangible experiment.

### Building the Experiment

**Wednesday, October 28**

We began assembling two jars for our experiment. The aim was to make the process and the effects of different inputs visible through a physical outcome. Working with the materials allowed us to move beyond discussing compost conceptually and start exploring how the experiment could communicate our ideas.

### Feedback and Reflection

**Thursday, October 29**

During feedback, we were encouraged to focus more on the visual presentation of our work and reduce our reliance on written or verbal explanations. This made us reconsider how effectively the experiment communicated its ideas through the materials and visual elements alone.

We also recognised that our research needed to be more visible in the final outcome. Rather than presenting research only as background information, we needed to find ways to incorporate it into the design itself. This helped us understand that the experiment should not only demonstrate a process but also communicate what we had learned through our research.
